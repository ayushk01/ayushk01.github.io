Shipping three codebases solo

Two years building Letsdrive's Flutter app, Express/Prisma backend, and Next.js admin alone — the judgment it takes to ship and maintain three codebases solo.

For roughly two years I was the entire engineering team on Letsdrive — a carpooling and peer-to-peer car-rental marketplace, two marketplaces living in one app. I built and still maintain three codebases for it: a Flutter mobile app, a TypeScript/Express/Prisma backend, and a Next.js admin dashboard. No teammate to review a PR, no one to hand the pager to, no architect above me to veto a bad call. Just me, a private repo per tier, and people depending on it in production.

People hear “solo” and picture either heroics or chaos. It’s neither. Doing it well is almost entirely about judgment exercised early — choosing structures your future self can still afford to maintain alone, many months and thirty-plus migrations later. That’s the real subject here.

Architecture is a letter to your future self

When you’re the only engineer, every shortcut is a loan you personally repay, with interest, at the worst possible time. So the governing question for every decision wasn’t “what’s fastest to write today” — it was “what will this cost me to change in a year, when I’ve forgotten exactly how it works.”

That single question drove the whole stack.

On mobile, I chose Flutter with GetX and a strictly modular structure — features split into self-contained units with their own controllers, bindings, and views. The point isn’t GetX as a religion; it’s that a solo developer needs to reopen a feature months later and reload its full context from one folder, not chase state and side effects across the whole app. Modularity is how you keep the codebase legible to a team of one whose memory has moved on.

On the backend, Prisma migrations were the non-negotiable. Thirteen models spanning both carpooling and rentals, evolved across thirty-plus migrations — every one of them a checked-in, ordered record of how the schema got to where it is. When there’s no second engineer to remember why a column exists, the migration history is the institutional memory. The schema doesn’t drift from reality, because the schema is the only way reality is allowed to change.

And the whole thing is typed end to end. Prisma generates types from the database; the Express layer speaks those types; the admin dashboard consumes the same shapes. A solo engineer cannot afford a class of bugs that a compiler can catch for free. End-to-end typing is the cheapest second pair of eyes you will ever hire — it just never sleeps and never goes on holiday.

The schema is the source of truth

Three codebases drift apart the instant they disagree about what a “trip” or a “booking” is. The discipline that keeps them coherent is boring and absolute: one source of truth, and it’s the database schema.

Everything radiates from there. The Prisma schema defines the nouns. The backend exposes them. Mobile and admin both consume the same contract. When a concept changes, it changes in the schema first, migrates forward, and the type errors that ripple outward are a feature — they tell me every place across three codebases that now needs attention. I don’t get to forget a consumer, because the types won’t compile until I’ve handled it.

The same logic applies to naming. An owner in the rental flow and a driver in the carpool flow are different roles, so they get different names — across the schema, the API, the Flutter models, and the admin tables. Consistent vocabulary across all three tiers means I never have to translate in my head when I jump between them. Coherence isn’t a document you write; it’s a set of constraints you refuse to violate, even at 2am.

Sequencing, and the courage to cut

Solo means there is no parallelism. I can’t build the backend and the app in the same week, so order is everything. The spine got built first — auth, the data model, the core trip and booking lifecycle — because everything else hangs off it, and a wrong call there is the most expensive to unwind. Real-time chat, live trip tracking, and Stripe payouts on both sides of the marketplace came once that spine could hold weight.

Cutting is the other half of the job, and the harder half. With one developer, scope is the only lever you actually control — you can’t add people, and you can’t compress reality. So every feature has to earn its maintenance cost forever, not just its build cost once. The features that survived are the ones a marketplace genuinely can’t function without. The flashy nice-to-haves lost, every time, to one question: am I willing to maintain this alone for the next two years?

Where solo wins, and where it bites

Solo’s real advantage is zero coordination cost and total context. Every decision is made by someone holding the entire system in his head at once — mobile, backend, and admin. There’s no meeting to align three teams on an API contract, no telephone game about what a field means. I change the schema, the backend, and both clients in one coherent motion, because they’re all me. Cross-cutting changes that would be a multi-team negotiation become an afternoon.

Where it bites is just as real, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. There’s no one to catch your blind spot, so the discipline — types, migrations, conventions — has to substitute for a reviewer. There’s no specialist to hand the gnarly Google Maps routing edge case or the Stripe webhook reconciliation to; you become competent at all of it or the product stalls. And every codebase you ship is one more you personally keep alive.

What it actually takes

The skill on display in Letsdrive isn’t writing Flutter, Express, or Next.js — plenty of people can do any one of those. It’s holding three production codebases coherent, alone, for two years, in a way that stays maintainable by one person who has to remember all of it. That’s not raw output. It’s judgment: choosing the structures, contracts, and constraints that let a single engineer not just ship software people depend on, but keep depending on it. Building it solo is the easy half. Maintaining it solo is the part that takes a complete engineer.
