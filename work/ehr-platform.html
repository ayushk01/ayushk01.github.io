Engineering deep-dive

I’ve spent about four years building and operating this platform — architecture, backend, frontend, mobile, the AI stack, and cloud — and I lead the engineering team that ships it. I’m one of the principal contributors to the core platform (roughly a quarter of the codebase). This page is the story of the decisions that made it work, told the way I’d tell another engineer.

The problem

An EHR earns its keep on the worst day of a clinic’s week: a full waiting room, a receptionist juggling walk-ins against the appointment book, a doctor with four minutes per patient who needs the last visit’s notes now. Software that is merely correct in a demo dies here. Anything slow, anything that loses a prescription, anything that makes the doctor look at the screen instead of the patient gets abandoned for the paper pad in the drawer — and it doesn’t come back.

The vendor-side problem is that every clinic genuinely is different. A dental chain needs tooth charting; a pediatric practice needs growth charts; a hospital needs in-patient flows a single-doctor clinic will never open. Each wants its own letterhead, its own record numbering, its own print formats. The two usual answers — a fork per client, or per-client conditionals threaded through one codebase — both rot, just at different speeds. Either way, the nth clinic costs nearly as much as the first, and the engineering team’s week is consumed by divergence instead of product.

The business pressure pushed even harder in the same direction: resellers wanted the product under their own brand, including internationally deployed builds. One-clinic-at-a-time was never going to survive that. The platform had to be one product — multi-tenant, white-label — where onboarding a clinic is a configuration exercise, not a release.

The architecture

There are three standard ways to isolate tenants: a database per tenant, a schema per tenant, or one shared database with a tenant key on every row that matters. We run the third: a single shared MySQL, with per-tenant isolation enforced through a tenant key carried by the data model end to end.

The trade-off is worth stating honestly, because it’s the decision the whole platform rests on. Database-per-tenant gives you isolation as an infrastructure guarantee — and makes every new clinic a provisioning event and every migration a fleet operation. Shared-database gives you the cheapest possible onboarding (a new tenant is rows, not infrastructure), migrations that run once, and cross-tenant operations you can actually reason about — at the price that isolation becomes an application-level discipline instead of an infrastructure guarantee. Every query must be scoped to the current organisation. Every new code path must resolve tenant context before it touches data. Get that discipline wrong once and tenant A sees tenant B’s patients.

So the design’s whole job is to make the disciplined path the easy path: the tenant is resolved once, at entry, and flows through everything. Theming, feature flags, permissions, and data access all read from that context rather than from scattered client checks. Correctness — the right clinician seeing only the right tenant’s data — is the default state, not a thing to remember case by case. The annotated diagram above shows the request flow.

Everything a clinic experiences as “different” is data, not code:

  • Modules per organisation — appointments, pharmacy, lab, in-patient, inventory, staffing — toggled per tenant, so a single-doctor clinic and a hospital run the same build.
  • Branding and print — logos, letterheads, prescription formats — configuration, rendered by one shared component system.
  • Record and series numbering — every clinic’s registration and receipt conventions, expressed as per-tenant series config instead of custom code.

That’s the principle: configuration, not forks. And the place it got tested hardest was white-label — which is the first of the hard parts below.

The hard parts

Holding one core against white-label sprawl

A white-label deployment is supposed to be a skin: the core product with a client’s brand, print templates, and store presence on top. Under deadline pressure, skins have a way of becoming forks. Over the years the platform accumulated dozens of custom-branded deployments — including internationally rebranded builds — and some of them had grown genuine fork-specific patches. The cost compounds quietly: a fix landed in core has to be ported n times, each fork drifts a little further, and eventually nobody can say with confidence what any given client is actually running.

I led the consolidation. First the estate itself: I migrated the company’s repositories into a single organisation and onboarded the team onto it, so the sprawl was at least visible in one place. Then the audit: fork by fork, what had actually diverged — which in most cases turned out to be branding and print templates, exactly what the skin layer was supposed to hold. The de-fork effort pulls anything real back into core behind configuration, and thins the forks back down to what they should have been. The rule I hold the line on now: if a client request can’t be expressed as configuration, that’s a gap in the core’s design — fix the core, don’t fork.

Interoperability with India’s national digital-health programme (NDHM / ABDM)

The platform implements FHIR R4 / HL7 interoperability for India’s National Digital Health Mission (NDHM / ABDM) — compliant document bundles, validation, and terminology — so clinics on the platform can participate in the national digital-health network.

What makes healthcare interop genuinely hard isn’t the transport; it’s that clinical reality is messier than any specification. FHIR is a huge spec in which nearly everything is optional — until a national profile and its validator say otherwise, at which point it’s all-or-nothing: a document bundle validates completely or is rejected completely. Your local data model, which grew up serving working clinics, has to be lifted into strict structures with coded terminology — and real prescriptions are free text, real diagnoses don’t arrive pre-coded, and the mapping decisions (make the local model stricter at the source, or translate at the boundary?) are the actual engineering. The work is unglamorous by design: build bundles, run them through validation, fix what fails, repeat — and know when a validation failure means your data model has been too lax all along.

The clinical AI & speech stack

Doctors document least exactly when documentation matters most: while they’re with the patient. The answer the platform ships is ambient documentation — an in-house AI medical scribe, speech-model fine-tuning, and speech-to-text (a Python stack I built and own) that turns the clinical conversation into structured notes inside the EHR.

Two things matter more than raw model quality here, and both are trust problems. Latency: a scribe that makes the doctor wait gets switched off within a week, so the pipeline is engineered around time-to-usable-draft, not benchmark scores. Trust: a scribe that is confidently wrong in a medical record is worse than no scribe, so output is always a draft the clinician reviews and signs — nothing the model produces auto-commits to the record. The speech models are fine-tuned for the domain, because generic speech-to-text fails on precisely the words that matter most in this setting: drug names, dosages, and clinical vocabulary as it’s actually spoken in real consultation rooms.

I wrote up the three product decisions that made the scribe trustworthy — and why latency, not model quality, decided adoption — in a short AI case study (PDF).

Scale & operations

Today the platform runs 2,000+ active clinics and hospitals, thousands of practising doctors, and hundreds of thousands of patients across 100+ medical specialties, processing tens of thousands of appointments a month, and sustains onboarding of dozens of new clinics a month — which only works because onboarding is configuration, not a release.

Operationally it’s the unfancy version of everything, done consistently:

  • Real-time where the clinic feels it — Socket.io pushes live appointment queues and updates, because a receptionist refreshing a stale schedule is how double-bookings happen.
  • Background work off the request path — Redis-backed job queues handle document generation, notifications, and the slow work, so clinical screens stay fast.
  • A staged deployment pipeline — changes go through staging before separated production frontend and backend fleets; I’m building out the pipeline that makes this boring.
  • Watching it — cloud monitoring and alerting on the fleet; I also drove a cloud cost-optimization initiative and a PDF-engine optimization, because prescriptions are printed artifacts and the print path is a clinical feature, not an afterthought.

Reliability, at this scale, is boring things done consistently. That’s the whole trick.

Mobile

Clinicians don’t live at desks, and patients never do. The platform ships React Native apps — per-specialty clinician prescription apps and patient apps — live on the App Store and Play Store: Hermes engine, native modules, Fastlane CI, and over-the-air updates so fixes reach clinics without waiting on store review. I led a major React Native version migration across the whole fleet of apps — the kind of work nobody applauds and everybody depends on.

What this proves

  • Zero to operated. Not a prototype and not a handoff — a multi-tenant SaaS designed, built, and operated in production for years, with the isolation model, the onboarding economics, and the operational scar tissue to show for it.
  • Healthcare interoperability depth. FHIR R4 / HL7 against a national programme’s (NDHM / ABDM) validator is rare, transferable expertise — the hard-won kind.
  • Shipped clinical AI. A medical scribe and fine-tuned speech models in daily clinical use, engineered around latency and trust — not a demo.
  • Leads the team. Architecture, conventions, code review, delivery, and the platform-level initiatives (estate consolidation, de-forking, deployment pipeline) that only happen when someone owns them.
